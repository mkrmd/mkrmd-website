Understanding the Knee

The body's largest joint — where stability, cushioning, and motion all have to work together.

The knee is a hinge joint that bends and straightens the lower leg, built from three bones: the femur (thigh bone), tibia (shin bone), and patella (kneecap). Four ligaments hold it stable in every direction. The ACL and PCL sit at the center of the joint and control the tibia's forward and backward movement, while the MCL and LCL run along the inner and outer sides to prevent side-to-side motion. These ligaments are commonly torn by abrupt twisting, hyperextension, or direct trauma — and of the four, the ACL is torn most often.

The knee is also a weight-bearing joint, and the meniscus is what makes that possible: a thick, specialized cushion that distributes force across the joint and creates a smooth surface for the bones to glide on. Because the meniscus has a blood supply only in its outer third, the location of a tear largely determines whether it can be repaired or needs to be trimmed. The ends of the bones are also capped with articular cartilage, which cushions and lubricates the joint; acute damage to it is painful and causes swelling, while long-term wear leads to arthritis — the loss of that cartilage surface and painful bone-on-bone contact.

A range of other problems can affect the knee as well, from patellar (kneecap) instability to complex multi-ligament injuries. Many can be diagnosed and treated with minimally invasive arthroscopy, which repairs the soft tissues surrounding the joint through small incisions. The conditions and procedures below span the full range of knee care, from meniscus repair to complex reconstruction.

Knee Surgery Philosophy

Restoration of stability and choosing the right procedure to return the knee to high function.

The knee is the largest joint in the body and one of the most mechanically complex — four major ligaments, two menisci, and intricate cartilage surfaces all working in concert to provide stability during cutting, jumping, and pivoting.

Dr. Ryan's approach to knee surgery begins with restoration of stability and function through the most appropriate procedure for each patient's injury and goals. This means repairing meniscus tears whenever possible — but only when the native tissue conditions support healing, not simply because technique allows it. Knowing when you should, not just because you can, is critical. Similarly, ligament reconstruction uses the best graft for that patient based on decades of research, and timing return-to-play decisions on objective strength and movement testing — not calendar dates alone.

Meniscus Preservation

When possible, repair is indicated and preferred. However, poor quality tissue with poor healing capacity can only lead to repeat surgery — thus doing the right surgery is more important than always attempting repair.

Optimal Reconstruction

Ligament reconstruction techniques that achieve isometry and best reflect the native biomechanics of the ligament being reconstructed produce superior long-term outcomes in high-demand athletes.

Objective Return-to-Play

Return to sport is based on objective criteria: strength testing, functional movement assessment, and psychological readiness — never calendar dates alone.

Cartilage Preservation

Cartilage transplant procedures, when indicated in the proper setting, can restore joint surface and delay or prevent the need for joint replacement in active young patients.

Revision Expertise

Failed ACL reconstructions and complex multiligament injuries require specialized expertise. Dr. Ryan's fellowship training includes management of the most challenging revision cases.

Conditions & Procedures

From simple meniscus repairs to complex multiligament reconstructions, Dr. Ryan offers comprehensive surgical management of knee injuries in athletes and active individuals.

ACL Tear

Complete or partial tears of the anterior cruciate ligament, usually during cutting or pivoting, causing instability and giving-way.

PCL Tear

Injury to the posterior cruciate ligament, often from a direct blow to the shin or forced hyperflexion, frequently part of a larger injury.

MCL Tear

Injury to the ligament on the inner knee, typically from a blow to the outside of the joint; most heal without surgery.

LCL Tear

Injury to the ligament on the outer knee, often part of a posterolateral corner injury that may require surgical repair.

Other Knee Ligament Injuries

Complex, multi-ligament injuries from high-energy trauma, requiring careful evaluation and coordinated reconstruction.

Meniscus Tear

Tears of the C-shaped cushion, common with twisting, causing pain along the joint line and mechanical catching or locking.

Articular Cartilage Injury

Focal damage to the cartilage surface of the knee, causing pain and swelling and, over time, a risk of arthritis.

Patellar (Kneecap) Dislocation

Instability in which the kneecap slips out of its groove, often driven by underlying anatomy and prone to recurrence.

Knee Arthritis

Progressive loss of cartilage — from wear, prior injury, or inflammation — leading to pain, stiffness, and reduced motion.

Knee Arthroscopy

Minimally invasive keyhole surgery used to diagnose and treat cartilage, meniscus, and ligament problems inside the knee.

Meniscus Repair & Meniscectomy

Preserving the meniscus with a repair when the tear allows, or trimming the damaged portion when it cannot heal.

ACL Reconstruction

Rebuilding a torn ACL with the ideal graft for each patient, using time-tested techniques proven in high-level athletes.

Other Ligament Repair/Reconstruction

Repair or reconstruction of the PCL, MCL, and LCL, including coordinated management of multi-ligament injuries.

MPFL Reconstruction

Rebuilding the ligament that stabilizes the kneecap to prevent recurrent dislocation, often with an arthroscopy to address joint damage.

Tibial Osteotomy

Realigning the bone to correct atypical anatomy that contributes to instability or uneven joint wear.

Cartilage Preservation Surgeries

Restoring focal cartilage defects with microfracture, osteochondral transplant, or cell-based techniques to delay arthritis.

Meniscus Transplant

Replacing a meniscus-deficient knee with a custom-sized donor graft to relieve pain and protect the joint.

Total Knee Replacement

Resurfacing an arthritic knee with metal and plastic components — the most reliable long-term treatment for advanced arthritis.

What to Expect

From initial injury through return to competition, here's your pathway back to full athletic participation.

Initial Visit

Diagnosis & Planning

Comprehensive exam including special tests for ligament stability, imaging review (MRI when indicated), and development of treatment plan. Dr. Ryan explains all options and expected timelines for return to sport.

Pre-Operative

Pre-Surgical Optimization

Motion is important, but reconstruction is immediate in most cases. Few require formal pre-rehabilitation if surgery is done soon after injury. However, if the knee is stiff, pre-operative therapy to regain motion may be recommended.

Surgery Day

Arthroscopic Procedure

Most knee procedures are outpatient arthroscopic surgery. ACL reconstruction typically takes 45-90 minutes. Patients go home the same day with detailed post-operative instructions and therapy protocols.

Weeks 1-12

Early Recovery & Strengthening

Full weight-bearing for most procedures, even with meniscus repair. Physical therapy begins on post-operative day 1, focusing on motion, swelling control, and progressive strengthening.

Months 3-6

Sport-Specific Training

Progressive return to running, cutting, and sport-specific movements. Objective testing includes strength measurements, hop tests, and movement analysis to ensure readiness for higher-level activities.

Months 6-9

Return to Competition

Return to full competition when objective criteria are met: symmetry goal between injured and uninjured sides, successful completion of sport-specific testing, and psychological readiness to compete.
