Other Knee Ligament Injuries

Along with the ACL, the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), medial collateral ligament (MCL), and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) make up the four main ligaments that connect the tibia (shin bone) to the femur (thigh bone) and stabilize the knee. Injuries to the PCL, MCL, and LCL are less common than ACL tears, and because a single impact can damage more than one ligament, a thorough examination is essential to reach a complete diagnosis.

The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) sits inside the knee, just behind the ACL, and prevents the shin bone from sliding too far backward relative to the thigh bone. PCL injuries are most often seen in contact-sport athletes and typically result from a direct blow to the front of the shin or from forced hyperflexion of the knee. They frequently occur as part of a multi-ligament injury. Most are treated non-operatively, though surgery may be considered when the resulting instability is severe.

The medial collateral ligament (MCL) runs along the inner side of the knee and stabilizes it against inward force. MCL injuries usually follow a direct blow to the outside of the knee or an abrupt pivoting or twisting motion, and are graded from I to III based on severity. The majority heal with non-operative care; repair is generally reserved for severe tears, particularly those that occur alongside other ligament injuries.

The lateral collateral ligament (LCL) runs along the outer side of the knee and keeps it from bending outward. LCL injuries typically result from a blow to the inner knee or a sudden change in direction, and — like MCL injuries — are common in contact sports and skiing. The LCL is often torn together with other structures at the back and outside of the knee, an injury known as a posterolateral corner (PLC) injury. Surgical repair is strongly recommended for PLC injuries, especially when the ACL or PCL is also involved, because non-operative treatment tends to leave the knee persistently unstable.

In isolation, PCL, MCL, and LCL injuries can often be managed non-operatively with physical therapy, bracing, activity modification, anti-inflammatory medication, ice, and occasionally injections, with surgery considered case by case. When several ligaments are injured at once, however, it signals a much more severe injury — one in which the knee has often shifted partially or completely out of place. In these situations, close attention to the nerves and blood vessels behind the knee is critical, and surgical stabilization is usually recommended, with the specific procedure dictated by which ligaments are involved.

Expert treatment for knee ligament injuries

Because a single injury can involve more than one ligament, a careful evaluation is essential to reach the full diagnosis and determine whether non-operative care or surgical stabilization will best restore your knee.
