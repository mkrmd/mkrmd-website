Hip Preservation & Arthroscopy

For hip pain in young, active patients, joint replacement is rarely the first answer. Dr. Ryan's practice is built around preserving the native hip — restoring normal anatomy, relieving pain, and keeping patients decades ahead of arthritis.

1,500+ Hip arthroscopies & preservation procedures
90%+ Patient satisfaction & return to sport
10+ Years of hip preservation expertise
2 Fellowship training programs (ASMI + Inselspital)
Why Hip Preservation?

The hip is irreplaceable. Almost always, it's worth preserving.

The hip is a deep, mechanically demanding joint, and small deviations from normal anatomy can create outsized symptoms and accelerate cartilage wear.

For decades, the only answer to progressive hip pain was replacement. Today, the evidence is clear: in the right patient, preserving the native hip produces better long-term function, delays or prevents replacement altogether, and lets young, active people return to the sports and activities that define them.

Dr. Ryan trained in hip arthroscopy at the American Sports Medicine Institute and in open hip preservation surgery — surgical hip dislocation, periacetabular osteotomy (PAO), and femoral osteotomies — at Inselspital in Bern, Switzerland. That combined training matters because the hardest part of hip preservation is not the technique itself; it's knowing, for each patient, which operation is the right one.

Hip Preservation

  • Preserves your native hip joint
  • Maintains natural biomechanics
  • Delays or prevents replacement
  • Returns athletes to full activity
  • Lasts decades when successful
  • No implant-related limitations
  • Can still have replacement later if needed

Hip Replacement

  • Removes your natural joint
  • Artificial joint mechanics
  • Permanent solution for arthritis
  • Activity restrictions remain
  • May need revision in 15-20 years
  • No impact sports recommended
  • Irreversible procedure

Conditions & Procedures

From diagnosis through recovery, Dr. Ryan offers the full spectrum of hip preservation techniques — arthroscopic, open, and hybrid approaches tailored to your anatomy and your goals.

Femoroacetabular Impingement (FAI)

Abnormal contact between the femoral head and acetabulum, often caused by excess bone (cam lesion) or socket overcoverage (pincer lesion).

  • Groin pain with hip flexion
  • Pain with getting in/out of car
  • C-sign: cupping hand over hip
  • Limited internal rotation

Labral Tears

Tears in the fibrocartilage rim that deepens the hip socket and provides joint stability, often associated with FAI or trauma.

  • Deep groin aching
  • Sharp catching or clicking
  • Pain with pivoting movements
  • Mechanical symptoms

Hip Dysplasia

Shallow hip socket that inadequately covers the femoral head, leading to joint instability and accelerated cartilage wear.

  • Lateral hip pain
  • Groin pain with walking
  • Hip instability sensation
  • Activity-related symptoms

Core Muscle Injury / Sports Hernia

Weakness or tear in the muscles of the lower abdominal wall and inner thigh, common in cutting and kicking sports.

  • Lower abdominal pain
  • Pain with coughing/sneezing
  • Adductor muscle pain
  • Pain with acceleration

Gluteus Medius/Minimus Tears

Tears in the primary hip stabilizing muscles, often called the "rotator cuff of the hip."

  • Lateral hip pain
  • Pain with side-lying
  • Weakness with single leg stance
  • Trendelenburg gait

Cartilage Damage

Injury to the smooth cartilage surface of the hip joint, often associated with FAI or acetabular labral tears.

  • Deep aching pain
  • Mechanical catching
  • Stiffness after sitting
  • Progressive symptoms

Hip Arthroscopy

Minimally invasive technique using small portals to address FAI, labral tears, and cartilage damage while preserving joint anatomy.

  • 2-3 small incisions (~10mm each)
  • Same-day surgery
  • Faster recovery than open surgery
  • Return to sport typically 4-7 months

Labral Reconstruction

When labral tissue is too damaged to repair, reconstruction using cadaver tendon graft restores the critical hip seal and stability.

  • Uses cadaver tendon graft
  • Restores labral function
  • For revision cases or chronic tears
  • Excellent long-term outcomes

Periacetabular Osteotomy (PAO)

Surgical reorientation of the hip socket to improve coverage in patients with hip dysplasia, dramatically altering joint mechanics.

  • Corrects socket orientation
  • Performed through Bernese approach
  • Hospitalization 1-3 days
  • Weight-bearing restrictions 6 weeks

Femoral Osteotomy

Surgical correction of abnormal femoral anatomy, including excessive anteversion or neck-shaft angle abnormalities.

  • Addresses femoral-sided deformity
  • Often combined with arthroscopy
  • Restores normal hip mechanics
  • Prevents future impingement

Surgical Hip Dislocation

Safe surgical approach allowing 360-degree access to the hip joint while protecting blood supply to the femoral head.

  • Access for complex deformities
  • Preserves femoral head blood supply
  • Allows cartilage restoration
  • For cases not amenable to arthroscopy

Core Muscle Repair

Direct repair of the pubic plate and rebalancing of mechanical forces through adductor lengthening, addressing the root cause of core muscle dysfunction.

  • Identifies specific tear pattern
  • Direct repair of pubic plate
  • Adductor lengthening for force balance
  • Return to sport typically 8-16 weeks

What to Expect

From initial consultation through full recovery, here's your pathway back to the activities you love.

Initial Visit

Consultation & Imaging

Thorough history, focused physical exam, and review of imaging — often on the same day. Dr. Ryan will explain your diagnosis and outline all treatment options, starting with the most conservative.

Pre-Operative

Surgery Planning

If surgery is recommended, you'll receive detailed pre-operative instructions, physical therapy protocols, and a clear timeline for recovery. Dr. Ryan's team coordinates all imaging and pre-surgical requirements.

Surgery Day

Minimally Invasive Procedure

Most hip arthroscopy procedures are same-day surgery. PAO and other open procedures typically require a 1-3 day hospital stay. Dr. Ryan personally discusses the procedure with your family afterward.

Weeks 1-6

Early Recovery

Hip arthroscopy patients walk with crutches typically 2-6 weeks depending on procedure. PAO patients are partial weight-bearing for 6 weeks. Physical therapy starts immediately (post-op day 1) with range-of-motion and protection phases.

Months 2-4

Strengthening & Function

Progressive strengthening begins once healing is confirmed on X-ray. Patients begin sport-specific movements and functional training. Most return to full activity during this phase.

Long-term

Return to Activity

Return to sport is based on objective criteria: strength testing, functional movement assessment, and tissue healing timeline. Full return typically occurs between 4-9 months post-surgery, depending on the procedure and activity level.
