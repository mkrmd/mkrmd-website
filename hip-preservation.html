Hip Preservation & Arthroscopy

Understanding the Hip

A deep ball-and-socket joint where shape is everything.

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint. The socket — called the acetabulum — is part of the pelvis, shaped like a hemisphere, and forms the weight-bearing surface of the joint. It is lined with smooth cartilage that cushions and lubricates the hip, allowing fluid, painless motion. Around the rim of the socket runs a ring of specialized cartilage called the labrum, which deepens the joint, creates a seal that keeps the natural joint fluid in place, and adds stability. Because the labrum is rich in nerve fibers, a tear can be a significant source of pain.

The ball of the joint is the femoral head — the rounded top of the thigh bone (femur) — and it, too, is capped in smooth cartilage that glides against the socket. In a healthy hip, the socket covers the ball to just the right extent, and the ball is perfectly round.

Small deviations from that ideal shape cause problems. If the socket covers too much of the ball, the bones collide — a pattern called pincer impingement. If the ball isn't perfectly round, cam impingement occurs, and moving the hip becomes like forcing a square peg into a round hole. Together these make up femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), and both patterns can damage and tear the labrum and cartilage.

At the opposite extreme, a socket with too little coverage — a shallow socket — leaves the hip unstable and prone to wear. This is hip dysplasia, and like impingement, it can lead to labral and cartilage tears.

Labral tears often cause groin pain that worsens with high-demand activity — running, jumping, cutting, squatting, pivoting — as well as everyday tasks like sitting, climbing stairs, or putting on socks and shoes. Left untreated for years, the repeated injury to the labrum and cartilage can progress to osteoarthritis, the loss of the joint's cartilage surface. When arthritis becomes advanced, a hip replacement may be the only option — but caught early, impingement and dysplasia can often be corrected before arthritis sets in. Surgery aimed at that goal is called hip preservation.

Beyond the joint itself, a range of injuries affect the surrounding muscles, tendons, and ligaments — gluteus and hamstring tendon tears, snapping hip, bursitis, ligamentum teres tears, instability, and growth-plate conditions — each capable of causing pain and limiting function. The full range of hip conditions and the procedures that treat them is listed below.

Why Hip Preservation?

The hip is irreplaceable. Almost always, it's worth preserving.

The hip is a deep, mechanically demanding joint, and small deviations from normal anatomy can create outsized symptoms and accelerate cartilage wear.

For decades, the only answer to progressive hip pain was replacement. Today, the evidence is clear: in the right patient, preserving the native hip produces better long-term function, delays or prevents replacement altogether, and lets young, active people return to the sports and activities that define them.

Dr. Ryan trained in hip arthroscopy at the American Sports Medicine Institute and in open hip preservation surgery — surgical hip dislocation, periacetabular osteotomy (PAO), and femoral osteotomies — at Inselspital in Bern, Switzerland. That combined training matters because the hardest part of hip preservation is not the technique itself; it's knowing, for each patient, which operation is the right one.

Hip Preservation

  • Preserves your native hip joint
  • Maintains natural biomechanics
  • Delays or prevents replacement
  • Returns athletes to full activity
  • Lasts decades when successful
  • No implant-related limitations
  • Can still have replacement later if needed

Hip Replacement

  • Removes your natural joint
  • Artificial joint mechanics
  • Permanent solution for arthritis
  • Activity restrictions remain
  • May need revision in 15-20 years
  • No impact sports recommended
  • Irreversible procedure

Conditions & Procedures

From diagnosis through recovery, Dr. Ryan offers the full spectrum of hip preservation techniques — arthroscopic, open, and hybrid approaches tailored to your anatomy and your goals.

Hip Impingement (FAI)

Abnormal contact between the ball and socket — from a cam or pincer deformity — that damages the labrum and cartilage.

Labral Tear

A tear of the cartilage rim that seals and stabilizes the socket, often causing groin pain and catching.

Hip Dysplasia

A shallow socket that under-covers the ball, leaving the hip unstable and prone to accelerated wear.

Hip Instability

Excessive motion of the ball within the socket, from injury or ligament laxity, causing a sense of the hip giving way.

Snapping Hip Syndrome

A snapping or popping from a tendon catching over bone, at the front (internal) or side (external) of the hip.

Hamstring Tears

Tears of the hamstring tendons where they attach at the pelvis, causing buttock pain and weakness.

Gluteus Tendon Tear

A tear of the gluteus medius or minimus — the "rotator cuff of the hip" — causing lateral hip pain and weakness.

Abnormal Femoral Version

Excessive rotation of the thigh bone that alters hip mechanics and can contribute to pain and impingement.

Acetabular Retroversion

A socket rotated backward, causing a form of impingement and abnormal contact within the joint.

Legg-Calvé-Perthes

A childhood condition in which the femoral head temporarily loses its blood supply, affecting its shape as it heals.

Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE)

A slip of the femoral head through its growth plate in adolescents, requiring prompt treatment.

Avascular Necrosis (AVN)

Loss of blood supply to the femoral head, causing the bone to weaken and collapse over time.

Hip Arthritis

Progressive loss of the joint's cartilage surface, leading to pain, stiffness, and reduced motion.

Hip Arthroscopy

Minimally invasive keyhole surgery to treat FAI, labral tears, and cartilage damage while preserving the joint.

Labral Repair

Reattaching the torn labrum to the socket rim with sutures to restore the hip's seal and stability.

Labral Reconstruction

Rebuilding an irreparable labrum with a tendon graft to restore its seal and function.

Cam Resection (Femoroplasty)

Reshaping the aspherical femoral head to remove the cam deformity and eliminate impingement.

Pincer Resection (Acetabuloplasty)

Trimming an over-covering socket rim to correct pincer impingement.

Capsule Repair

Closing and reinforcing the hip capsule to restore stability after arthroscopy.

Gluteus Tendon Repair

Reattaching torn gluteus tendons to relieve lateral hip pain and restore strength.

Proximal Hamstring Repair

Reattaching torn hamstring tendons at the pelvis to restore strength and function.

Periacetabular Osteotomy (PAO)

Reorienting the socket to provide proper coverage of the ball in hip dysplasia.

Surgical Dislocation of the Hip (SDH)

A safe open approach giving full access to the joint to correct complex deformities.

Femoral Rotational Osteotomy

Correcting abnormal thigh-bone rotation to restore normal hip mechanics.

What to Expect

From initial consultation through full recovery, here's your pathway back to the activities you love.

Initial Visit

Consultation & Imaging

Thorough history, focused physical exam, and review of imaging — often on the same day. Dr. Ryan will explain your diagnosis and outline all treatment options, starting with the most conservative.

Pre-Operative

Surgery Planning

If surgery is recommended, you'll receive detailed pre-operative instructions, physical therapy protocols, and a clear timeline for recovery. Dr. Ryan's team coordinates all imaging and pre-surgical requirements.

Surgery Day

Minimally Invasive Procedure

Most hip arthroscopy procedures are same-day surgery. PAO and other open procedures typically require a 1-3 day hospital stay. Dr. Ryan personally discusses the procedure with your family afterward.

Weeks 1-6

Early Recovery

Hip arthroscopy patients walk with crutches typically 2-6 weeks depending on procedure. PAO patients are partial weight-bearing for 6 weeks. Physical therapy starts immediately (post-op day 1) with range-of-motion and protection phases.

Months 2-4

Strengthening & Function

Progressive strengthening begins once healing is confirmed on X-ray. Patients begin sport-specific movements and functional training. Most return to full activity during this phase.

Long-term

Return to Activity

Return to sport is based on objective criteria: strength testing, functional movement assessment, and tissue healing timeline. Full return typically occurs between 4-9 months post-surgery, depending on the procedure and activity level.
