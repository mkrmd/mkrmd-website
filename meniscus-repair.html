Meniscus Repair & Meniscectomy

The meniscus sits between the femur (thigh bone) and tibia (shin bone), cushioning the joint and reducing stress on the knee's cartilage. When it tears, the two main surgical options are a repair — suturing the tear back together — or a meniscectomy, trimming away the damaged portion. Which one is possible depends largely on the meniscus's blood supply.

The meniscus has its own blood supply, but it is uneven: only the outer third receives ample blood, the middle third receives a minimal amount, and the inner third receives none at all, relying instead on nutrients absorbed from the joint fluid. This matters because blood supply is what allows a torn meniscus to heal. As a result, tears in the outer portion can heal and are amenable to repair, while tears in the inner portion cannot heal and are instead trimmed away.

A meniscus repair uses sutures to reattach the torn portion to the intact tissue. In a common all-inside technique, specialized sutures are passed through the torn segment into the healthy meniscus, grasping the tissue just behind it, and then cinched down tightly. After a repair, intensive, protocol-driven physical therapy is essential to achieve a good result and to allow a safe return to full activity.

When a tear is in the inner portion, or is shredded and cannot heal, the damaged tissue is removed instead — a procedure called a meniscectomy, performed during a knee arthroscopy. Using fine cutting and shaving tools, the torn flaps are excised and the remaining rim is contoured as smoothly as possible to recreate the natural curve of the meniscus.

Most meniscectomies preserve much of the meniscus, but occasionally a large portion must be removed. Because the meniscus protects the joint, removing more of it increases the force on the knee's cartilage and can raise the risk of arthritis later in life. For that reason, every effort is made to preserve as much healthy meniscus as possible.

Preserving the meniscus

Whenever a tear can be repaired, preserving the meniscus protects the knee for the long term. A specialized evaluation can determine whether your tear is repairable and guide the surgical approach most likely to keep you active.
